Ten prisoners are told the rules in advance and can agree a strategy. Then each gets a hat in one of **ten colours**. Colours can repeat, and any combination is possible, including all ten the same.

Each prisoner can see the other nine hats but **not their own**. At a signal, all ten write down a guess for their own hat colour **at the same time**. No communication is allowed once the hats are on.

If **at least one** prisoner guesses correctly, they all go free.

**Is there a strategy that guarantees freedom, whatever the hats are?** And how many prisoners will be correct?

<Spoiler>

Yes. The strategy guarantees that **exactly one** prisoner is correct, every time.

Number the colours **0 to 9** and number the prisoners **0 to 9**. Let T be the sum of **all ten** hat numbers, taken modulo 10 (so just the last digit).

Nobody knows T, because nobody sees their own hat. But each prisoner can assume a value for it. The strategy is:

**Prisoner k assumes T = k**, and guesses whichever colour would make that true.

Concretely, prisoner k adds up the nine hats they can see, gets some total V, and guesses the colour c where V + c ends in the digit k.

Why it works: the real T is *some* digit from 0 to 9. Exactly one prisoner assumed that digit, and for that prisoner, "the colour that makes the total equal T" is their actual hat colour. Every other prisoner assumed a wrong total, so they're all wrong.

And why you can't do better: whatever the strategy, each prisoner on their own is guessing a hat they can't see, so across all possible hat assignments each prisoner is right exactly 1 time in 10. That makes the average number of correct guesses exactly **1**. A strategy that never gets zero must therefore always get exactly one.

Error-correcting codes use the same trick: split the possibilities so that each check covers exactly one case.

</Spoiler>
