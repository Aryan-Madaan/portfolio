Ignore leap years and assume every day of the year is equally likely to be someone's birthday. You put people in a room one at a time.

**How many people do you need before it's more likely than not that at least two of them share a birthday?** Most people guess somewhere near 183, which is half of 365. Make your guess, then work out the real answer.

<Spoiler>

The answer is **23 people**, which gives a probability of about **50.7%**.

The intuition for 183 comes from asking "will someone share *my* birthday?" That's a much harder event. The question here is whether *any* two people among all of them match, and the number of pairs grows far faster than the number of people.

Count pairs instead. With 23 people there are 23 × 22 / 2 = **253 pairs**, and each pair has a 1/365 chance of matching. That's a lot of chances.

To get the exact figure, it's easier to calculate the opposite: the probability that **nobody** shares a birthday.

- The second person must avoid the first person's date: 364/365
- The third must avoid both: 363/365
- And so on, down to the 23rd: 343/365

Multiply them together:

P(no match) = (365/365) × (364/365) × (363/365) × … × (343/365) ≈ **0.4927**

So P(at least one match) = 1 − 0.4927 ≈ **0.5073**.

It climbs quickly after that. With 30 people it's about 70%, with 50 about 97%, and with 70 people it's above 99.9%.

The same maths is why hash collisions show up much sooner than you'd expect, and it's the basis of the "birthday attack" in cryptography.

</Spoiler>
